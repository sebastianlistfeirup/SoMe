# Pixel and Parameters recipe cards

Twenty reusable English formats. Durations are editorial targets, not platform limits. Every required placeholder must be filled from evidence or actual footage before production.

### GF01 The Amiga Corner

**Promise:** show why one old game is worth understanding now. **Inputs:** original-platform gameplay, official or archival description, identifiable historical reception, modern-source check, one corroborated historical detail and a current access route if recommending play.

**Hook:** “This tiny detail is why [game] stayed with people.” Avoid asserting that Sebastian personally remembers it unless he says so.

**Long-form shape, 5–8 minutes:** 0:00–0:15 show the memorable moment; 0:15–0:40 identify game/platform and the episode's question; 0:40–1:30 explain the loop through a complete action; 1:30–2:15 give spoken reception and short archival context; 2:15–4:30 examine two strengths and one aged element using footage; 4:30–5:30 explain what today's viewer may enjoy; optional extra time for a sourced production story. Adapt the duration to the argument.

**Short derivative, 45–60 seconds:** one moment, one historical fact, the compact reception/coverage line, one specific question. Do not squash the entire history into the Short.

**Visual rule:** retain the game's intended proportions and readable playfield. Decorative frames must not consume the action. **Learning question:** do viewers stay for the historical explanation, or mainly for the gameplay reveal? Compare matched episodes before changing the balance.

### GF02 Great Games That Are Not New

**Promise:** a curated rediscovery with a specific reason to play. **Inputs:** 3–5 games with a meaningful shared benefit, exact edition/platform, footage illustrating that benefit, official descriptions and required reception records.

**Hook:** “You do not need a new release to get [specific experience]. These [number] games deliver it.” Avoid universal claims about all new releases.

**Long-form shape, 4–7 minutes:** 15-second premise, then 40–70 seconds per game: distinctive moment → what the player does → why it fits the list → critic and Steam reception → one trade-off. End by matching each game to a different type of viewer. The list is a recommendation, not a disguised ranking unless criteria actually produce an order.

**Short derivative:** one title or a three-game set with sufficient speaking time. **Selection rule:** define the age window against original release or the edition shown, explicitly; “last ten years” must be recalculated on publication day.

**Possible wrappers:** “Games for One Brilliant Decision,” “Older Sci-Fi Games That Still Feel Strange,” “Games for People Who Miss Planning.” **Learning question:** which audience benefit drives saves or qualified comments?

### GF03 The Ranked Top 10 15 or 20

**Promise:** help viewers choose through a transparent editorial ranking. **Inputs:** candidate pool larger than final list, published scope, eligibility rules, fresh reception, evidence for every differentiator and footage for every entry.

**Hook:** “These are our [number] picks for [audience and situation], ranked by [two or three understandable criteria].” Use “our picks” unless claiming a measured external ranking with a reproducible method.

**Shape:** 20–30 seconds of scope and a preview; 25–45 seconds per entry; a slightly longer top three; a concise final recommendation. Top 10 is usually a several-minute video; Top 20 needs more room. Count the actual spoken words and do a timed read instead of hardcoding duration. Every entry contains spoken reception. Create Shorts from individual entries with self-contained context.

**Criteria example for handheld games:** controls, text readability, suitability for interrupted sessions, and observed performance on the named device. Verified compatibility is supporting evidence, not an FPS or battery test. Valve defines compatibility categories, but the channel still needs device-specific evidence for its own performance claims. [G15]

**Guardrails:** don't list all games as winners; don't invent decimal ranking precision; don't silently mix Switch versions with emulated originals. **Learning question:** where does retention drop in the countdown, and does the opening promise match the actual selection?

### GF04 Games Under a Real Budget

**Promise:** an actionable shortlist within a clearly defined purchase budget. **Inputs:** official store or authorised retailer, exact edition, region/currency, current price, promotion end if provided, reception, own or appropriately licensed footage.

**Hook:** “[Budget] can buy you [specific kind of experience]—these prices were checked for [region] on [date].” Never use a future or expired sale as current.

**Shape, 60–90 seconds for up to three games:** 5-second budget definition; 15–22 seconds per game including receipt-style price display, gameplay benefit, spoken reception and key caveat; 5-second reminder that prices can change. A longer list becomes a longer video.

**Hard gate:** if any entry breaches the budget at final review, replace the title, change the premise, or remove the entry. Do not count subscription access as ownership or a base-game price as including required DLC. State whether taxes and platform subscriptions are included when material.

**Fallback:** “Older Games Worth Wishlisting” is a valid evergreen version if price evidence is unavailable, provided the title and script no longer promise a current deal. **Learning question:** compare useful click-through or saves, not raw views alone.

### GF05 The Handheld Reality Check

**Promise:** show how a particular game or device behaves in a named real use case. **Inputs:** exact hardware, OS/driver/build, settings, footage of controls and readable UI, recorded performance if claimed, review sources, and test limitations.

**Hook:** “The important handheld test isn't the explosion. It's whether you can read this menu and make this move.” This is an editorial point, not an objective universal rule.

**Shape, 3–6 minutes:** show the actual task; identify device/settings; examine input, readability, performance and practical comfort; deliver scoped game reception; explain who the result suits. If no hands-on data exists, title the piece “What the Specifications and Reviews Tell Us,” and label external findings.

**Short derivative, 45–60 seconds:** one test and one conclusion. Do not claim battery life from a short observation, infer FPS from a store badge, or treat Windows handhelds and SteamOS devices as interchangeable. Manufacturer specifications and independent measurements have different roles.

**Series extensions:** “One Game, Two Devices,” “The Tiny Text Test,” “Pause and Resume Check,” “Can This Strategy Game Work Without a Mouse?” **Learning question:** which practical test predicts qualified questions and returning viewers?

### GF06 Emulation First Session

**Promise:** take a viewer from a defined starting point to one working, authorised game or homebrew demo. **Inputs:** one emulator/platform/version, official installation documentation, an authorised BIOS/ROM route where needed, tested settings, reproducible capture and a troubleshooting fallback.

**Hook:** “Let's get one game running properly before adding a hundred.” **Shape, 5–9 minutes:** show final result; specify device and version; explain which components are required; install from official sources; configure one input device; load permitted content; check picture, sound, saving and reopening; solve one likely problem. Show each real step, not slides describing hypothetical clicks.

Emulator software, BIOS files and game data need separate provenance. The MAME project's documentation explicitly rejects treating “abandonware” as a permission shortcut, and Amiga Forever documents licensed ROM/OS options and restrictions. Verify the relevant licence and jurisdiction rather than asserting that owning a cartridge or computer universally authorises copying. [G16][G17]

If the guide recommends a named game, include its required reception or the retro exception line. A setup guide using an authorised homebrew demo need not invent Metacritic coverage. **Learning question:** do viewers reach the first successful launch, and which step generates repeat support questions?

All durations below are editorial targets. Adjust the script to the actual spoken pace and available evidence. Templates are in English, and braces denote fields to fill from verified research or recorded tests.

### AI-F01 The launch translated into a decision

**Best use:** A new model, new access tier or meaningful feature update. **Viewer outcome:** Know whether to try it now, wait, or keep the existing setup. **Suggested format:** 50–80 second vertical explainer, with a 5–8 minute expanded comparison only after a real test.

**Hook:** “{Model} just changed. Here is the part that matters if you {specific job}.”

**Inputs:** Official announcement, exact model ID, release date, availability, current pricing or limits when relevant, one audience task, and the previous workflow. Record missing access explicitly.

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–4 seconds: show the task’s desired output, labelled illustrative if it predates the new model. 4–10: show the dated release heading with a tight highlight. 10–24: explain one documented change using a short cursor-led comparison. 24–43: show the actual test result if captured; otherwise show the test the channel proposes and label it “Test plan.” 43–60: state the caveat and audience decision. 60–70: ask a specific useful question, such as “Which task should the comparison include?”

**Narration skeleton:** “{Provider} says {documented change}. Access currently requires {condition}. That makes {task} worth testing. The useful comparison is {success criterion}, not simply whether the answer looks impressive. {Observed result OR explicit not-tested statement}. For {viewer type}, my next step would be {bounded action}.”

**Distinctive value:** Translate release language into a decision. Do not read a benchmark table over stock server footage. Do not call a vendor chart the channel’s result. Use the channel’s own footage where possible; promotional clips require a recorded reuse basis and must be visibly identified.

**Learning question:** Does a task-specific hook retain more of the relevant audience than a model-name-only hook? Compare matched launch episodes, without assuming different releases attracted identical audiences.

### AI-F02 One useful task from start to finish

**Best use:** MCP, a coding agent, a tool integration or a neglected app feature. **Viewer outcome:** Reproduce one small result. **Suggested format:** 60–100 second demonstration and a linked 6–10 minute full tutorial.

**Hook:** “By the end of this, your assistant will {one concrete action}.”

**Inputs:** Current official client documentation, verified tool/server identity, a disposable example project, exact app version and a visible success condition. MCP is a protocol for connecting applications to external systems; which features work depends on the actual client and server. [A05]

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–5: show the finished example. 5–12: name the prerequisite and exact client. 12–35: show the few essential setup actions with cropped readable UI. 35–55: execute the request and leave enough continuous footage to establish what happened. 55–72: inspect the output against the success condition. 72–85: show one common failure and the recovery path. If setup needs more time, use the long tutorial rather than speed-reading commands.

**Narration skeleton:** “I’m using {app version} with {tool}. Give it access to {specific scope}. Ask: {short task}. The useful check is here: {visible output}. If you see {error}, check {documented cause}. You now have {achieved outcome}.” Use “I’m using” only after recording that setup.

**Visual rule:** Keep the cursor purposeful. Enlarge the active control, shorten dead waiting with an explicit time jump, and show the output in the real destination. A three-box animation may explain the relationship, but it cannot replace the demonstration.

**Stop condition:** If the connection is unavailable, produce a clearly labelled conceptual explainer or wait for capture; never generate a fake successful interface.

### AI-F03 The local AI fit test

**Best use:** “Can my PC run this?”, first Ollama setup, long-context failures, offloading or a hardware upgrade. **Viewer outcome:** Determine whether a specific workload fits and runs acceptably. **Suggested format:** 70–100 second short, or 7–12 minute benchmark guide.

**Hook:** “Before you buy a new GPU, run these three checks.”

**Inputs:** Exact model and quantization, backend version, OS and driver, available memory, actual context length and the intended task. Verify support for the precise hardware and operating system; Ollama’s published compatibility lists differ by backend and OS. [A15]

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–5: show the stalled or successful task. 5–20: inspect loaded model, memory and offloading. 20–40: run a fixed prompt at the intended context. 40–60: record first-answer delay and useful completion, not only scrolling speed. 60–80: try one justified change. 80–95: make a limited recommendation for the tested setup.

**Narration skeleton:** “This is {model}, at {quantization}, on {machine}. Here is the actual memory use. Here is the context length. And here is how long a correct answer takes. {Measured result}. Change {one variable} before spending money.”

**Accuracy guard:** Parameter count alone does not determine total runtime memory. A rough weight-only estimate may help illustrate a concept, but the real budget also includes cache, runtime allocations and other processes. Do not imply that active MoE parameters equal all resident weights. Do not convert NPU TOPS into tokens per second.

**Useful deliverable:** A small repeatable measurement sheet, with empty fields rather than invented benchmarks. Avoid universal “a 70B model needs X GB” claims without specifying representation and runtime.

### AI-F04 The fair task duel

**Best use:** Two models, two effort levels, or local versus cloud. **Viewer outcome:** Choose for a specific job. **Suggested format:** 75–110 second result summary plus a detailed evidence video.

**Hook:** “Same bug. Same files. Which model gets us to a working fix?”

**Inputs:** A fixed task snapshot, acceptance criteria written before testing, available tools, equal intervention rules, model versions, effort and token settings, time limit and cost accounting method.

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–6: show the failing behavior. 6–15: display the simple rules. 15–38: intercut meaningful decisions from both runs; keep clocks labelled if compressed. 38–65: run the same independent checks. 65–85: show pass/fail, elapsed time, intervention count and measured cost. 85–100: state the scope of the verdict.

**Narration skeleton:** “For this task, success means {criterion}. Both get {same input} and {same tool allowance}. {A} did {observed result}; {B} did {observed result}. Across {n} recorded runs, {summary}. That tells us {narrow decision}; it does not rank every capability.”

**Accuracy guard:** A single run is a demonstration. Repeated runs are still a small test unless the sample warrants more. Include failed attempts. Report time to accepted outcome; distinguish input, cached input, output, reasoning charges if exposed, subscription allocation and API prices. A subscription test cannot honestly claim a measured API invoice it never incurred.

**Learning question:** Which audience cares more about accepted-result time, total cost or correctness? Ask this after the evidence, then use answers to design a later comparison.

### AI-F05 Build a tiny agent team

**Best use:** Combining Claude, Codex, Qwen or other subscriptions. **Viewer outcome:** Understand one useful handoff with clear ownership. **Suggested format:** 90 second demonstration and a 8–12 minute reproducible workflow.

**Hook:** “Three assistants only help if you stop giving them the same job.”

**Inputs:** One small task, three bounded roles, written handoff fields, one project owner and a shared acceptance check. Pick roles based on tested ability and actual access, not a permanent brand hierarchy.

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–7: show the finished feature and one original failure. 7–20: identify planner, builder and reviewer. 20–45: show a concise plan transferred to the builder, then a real diff. 45–68: show the reviewer finding a specific issue. 68–85: show the owner accepting or rejecting it and the test result. 85–95: show the final handoff note.

**Narration skeleton:** “{Role A} produces {artifact}. {Role B} changes only {scope}. {Role C} checks {criteria}. The owner resolves disagreements and decides what ships. Here is the point where another agent helped: {observed issue}.”

**Anti-chaos rule:** Do not allow several agents to edit the same files without coordination. Do not send every previous transcript to every model. Preserve decisions, current state, files changed and unresolved questions in the handoff.

**Useful deliverable:** A reusable five-field handoff: objective, constraints, approved files, evidence, next action. The episode must show an actual improvement, or honestly conclude that one agent was sufficient for this task.

### AI-F06 A public question becomes an investigation

**Best use:** Recurring confusion, a provocative demo or a claimed regression. **Viewer outcome:** Get evidence for a question people are asking. **Suggested format:** 60–90 second test or 6–10 minute investigation.

**Hook:** “People are asking whether {claim}. Here is a small test we can actually check.”

**Inputs:** The original public claim, chart, demo or thread; its exact URL and context; independent primary documentation; and a testable question. A Reddit thread is required only when Reddit is the actual origin. Remove usernames from casual visual screenshots unless attribution is necessary. Do not turn comments into a representative poll.

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–7: present the question in the channel’s own words. 7–18: distinguish what the post claims from what is known. 18–50: show the test and its limitations. 50–72: reveal observed results. 72–88: answer the narrow question and name what remains unresolved.

**Narration skeleton:** “The discussion raises {question}. That is different from proving {stronger claim}. I tested {bounded setup}. {Outcome}. The evidence supports {limited conclusion}. To answer the bigger question, we would need {additional evidence}.”

**Accuracy guard:** Do not use a generated moderator summary as a substitute for reading the thread. Upvotes are a discovery signal, not a truth score. Removed posts remain marked removed; third-party summaries cannot silently restore them as primary evidence.

**Production fallback:** If a proper experiment is too expensive, explain the missing information and give a checklist for evaluating the claim. Label the piece analysis, not test results.

### AI-F07 The hardware decision in context

**Best use:** A GPU, mini PC, AI laptop, handheld or documented offer. For an AI software purchase, use the subscription variant below. **Viewer outcome:** Decide whether a particular device fits an identified task and budget. **Suggested format:** 60–100 second shortlist plus a detailed buying guide.

**Hook:** “This machine makes sense for {workload}. Here is the tradeoff to check first.”

**Inputs:** Exact SKU, memory arrangement and capacity, OS, backend support, storage, power and cooling constraints, availability, retailer region, currency, tax, shipping and timestamp. A news article is not proof of current inventory. A processor specification is not proof of the configuration sold in a particular listing.

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–6: show the workload on the device if recorded. 6–22: show the configuration and the relevant constraint. 22–48: compare against two real alternatives using the same task and evidence quality. 48–70: explain who benefits and who should prioritize another characteristic. 70–90: show the dated price and exact conditions if a deal is claimed.

**Narration skeleton:** “At {verified delivered price in region}, this {exact configuration} offers {relevant documented capability}. Our test shows {result}, or: we have not tested this machine. For {job}, check {deciding constraint}. My shortlist would change if {condition}.”

**Accuracy guard:** Keep manufacturer claims, independent measurements and the channel’s measurements distinct. Do not promise that shared-memory capacity matches dedicated-GPU speed. “Best” must specify the use case, budget, eligible market and comparison pool.

**Expiry:** Recheck price and stock at publication; remove the bargain hook if the offer expired. Keep an evergreen hardware explainer variant ready.

### AI-F08 One failed attempt made useful

**Best use:** A broken agent run, poor generated code, unreadable output, hallucinated citation or an incorrect automation. **Viewer outcome:** Recognize the failure and apply one fix. **Suggested format:** 45–75 second short.

**Hook:** “This looked finished. One check showed it was wrong.”

**Inputs:** An actual before state, a reproducible failure, the narrow change made and the after check. Use synthetic sample records for demonstrations involving private data.

**Timed beats and shots:** 0–5: show the visible problem. 5–18: reveal the check that caught it. 18–40: show the precise fix. 40–58: repeat the same check. 58–70: state the reusable lesson and the remaining limit.

**Narration skeleton:** “The model produced {output}. It looked plausible, but {check} failed. I changed {one thing}. The same check now shows {observed result}. Keep {lesson} in your workflow.”

**Integrity rule:** Do not stage a failure and imply it happened naturally. A constructed teaching example is fine when labelled. Do not conclude that the whole model is unreliable from one failed run, or claim a fix generalizes beyond what was checked.

**Visual rule:** Favor the actual failure, diff and retest over animated warning icons. Show enough of the output for the audience to understand why the correction matters.

### Adapters for lists and software purchases

For the numbered AI pitches, apply a ranked-list adapter before the base recipe: state the audience, candidate pool and selection criteria; assign a reasonable per-entry budget; use the base recipe as each entry's evidence structure; and conclude with a useful decision. The single-task timeline does not govern an entire Top 10 or Top 20. A checklist may order actions by practical sequence rather than claim one tool is objectively best.

The AI-F07 software purchase variant replaces hardware SKU, memory and cooling fields with exact plan, model access, included usage, tool permissions, API versus subscription billing, cancellation terms where relevant, and observed cost per useful task. Recheck current terms and prices. Do not require a hardware specification for a software-only comparison.


### XF01 One mechanic explained

**Distinctive promise:** teach the viewer to recognize one satisfying decision, using a complete moment rather than a general review.

**Inputs:** one identifiable game and version; a clip containing setup, decision and consequence; a replay or second example; official description; required reception; a verified rule explanation.

**Hook variants:** “Watch the enemy on the left. My next move changes what it hits.” Or: “This looks like a damage problem. Watch what happens when the position changes.” Use a hook only when the footage demonstrates it.

**Timed narrative and shot map, 60–75 seconds:** 0–5 show the unresolved position. 5–14 identify the game and the immediate objective. 14–29 replay the decision in normal time, highlighting only the relevant unit or control. 29–43 show the consequence and explain the rule in one sentence. 43–59 give the compact spoken critic and Steam reception, or truthful coverage exception, over a second relevant clip. 59–70 show a contrasting position where the same move would fail. End with the practical takeaway.

**Evidence:** distinguish an observed interaction from a universal rule. If the manual or test does not establish all circumstances, say “in this position.” Record the footage timecodes supporting the explanation.

**Adaptations:** YouTube adds two progressively harder positions; Instagram keeps one readable decision; Facebook adds the game's context; TikTok opens at the choice and promptly resolves it.

**CTA:** “Which move would you have tried first?” **Learning question:** does showing the consequence before explaining the rule improve understanding without reducing curiosity?

### XF02 Then versus now

**Distinctive promise:** compare how two games solve the same design problem, while separating resemblance from documented historical influence.

**Inputs:** two or three matched games, versions, eras, display configurations or progression stages; an explicit comparison type; footage illustrating the same player problem; required descriptions and reception for every featured game; evidence for any claimed historical relationship.

**Hook variants:** “Two games, decades apart, ask you the same difficult question.” Or: “The old version explains this with movement. The newer one uses this interface.” Specify the actual difference after inspecting footage.

**Timed narrative and shot map for a two-item short, 90–120 seconds:** 0–8 intercut the matching decisions. 8–20 define the comparison and identify both games. 20–42 let the older interaction play through, then give its spoken reception. 42–66 show the newer interaction and its reception. 66–91 compare one gain and one tradeoff using matched freeze frames or replay. 91–108 state who might prefer each approach. Reserve the ending for the limited conclusion.

**Evidence:** match difficulty, game mode and task where practical. Never claim the newer designer copied or was inspired by the older title without a suitable primary statement. Otherwise say “they offer a useful comparison.” Preserve different review eras and scales; do not declare a winner by subtracting unrelated ratings.

**Adaptations:** YouTube supports a fuller three-question comparison. Short platforms take one self-contained contrast, with audible reception for both games; extend runtime if necessary. Instagram can add a companion comparison carousel.

For a three-way comparison or detailed display/progression study, expand the runtime and record every changed condition; the older/newer example is one possible arrangement.

**CTA:** “Which approach makes the decision clearer?” **Learning question:** do viewers respond to the design tradeoff, or mainly to recognizing the older game?

### XF03 What the manual promised

**Distinctive promise:** test one specific statement from an original manual or box description against recorded play.

**Inputs:** a legible, authenticated source for the correct edition; bibliographic details; a short relevant quotation or faithful paraphrase; original-platform footage; reception research; permission or an appropriate reviewed basis for any reproduced image.

**Hook variants:** “The manual promises {brief verified phrase}. Let's see what that means in play.” Or: “This sentence explains the game better than its opening screen does.” The latter is an editorial judgment to demonstrate.

**Timed narrative and shot map, 75–100 seconds:** 0–8 show the tested gameplay moment. 8–22 identify the manual, edition and promise. Use a brief attributed excerpt, not a full page of unreadable text. 22–49 show the action that tests the promise, including necessary setup. 49–66 explain what the result supports and what it leaves open. 66–84 deliver the spoken reception and period context. 84–96 return to the opening moment with the newly understood rule.

**Evidence:** separate literal instructions, promotional language and the episode's interpretation. A modern rerelease's description cannot silently stand in for an original manual. Never invent a quote when the scan is unclear; paraphrase the verified meaning or choose another passage.

**Adaptations:** YouTube adds a second passage and a period-review comparison. Vertical cuts focus on one promise. Facebook briefly establishes the machine and year; Instagram's companion cards preserve attribution and reception.

**CTA:** “Which old manual actually taught you something useful?” **Learning question:** does the archival passage make viewers understand the footage better than a purely modern explanation?

### XF04 Myth into a small test

**Distinctive promise:** make one AI misconception falsifiable through a controlled change. This is a compact teaching experiment, distinct from a general model duel or a report about Reddit sentiment.

**Inputs:** a precisely worded claim; current relevant documentation; one fixed task; two recorded runs differing in one declared setting; predetermined success checks; complete outcomes and attempt count.

**Hook variants:** “Does {one setting} actually change {observable outcome}? Here is the smallest test.” Or: “Before repeating this advice, let's change one variable and look.”

**Timed narrative and shot map, 75–100 seconds:** 0–7 show the question beside the result being measured, without revealing a fabricated outcome. 7–20 state the claim and controls. 20–42 show the baseline and its check. 42–64 change the one variable and repeat. 64–85 compare the actual outputs in the same destination or test. 85–98 state what this example supports and what would require more runs.

**Evidence:** distinguish invalidating an absolute claim with a genuine counterexample from estimating how often an effect occurs. Record model, runtime, prompt, context, hardware when relevant, timing method and retries. Do not attribute differences to the setting when another material factor changed.

**Adaptations:** YouTube includes the uncut test and uncertainty discussion. Instagram presents the controlled change clearly; TikTok begins at the measurement; Facebook explains the practical implication. None turns the result into a universal buying recommendation.

**CTA:** “Which condition should we change next?” **Learning question:** can viewers correctly describe the variable and limited conclusion after watching?

### XF05 One task from start to finish

**Distinctive promise:** show the complete life of one useful artifact, including its handoff into the application where it must work. This complements setup tutorials by testing what happens after an assistant says the task is finished.

**Inputs:** a small practical task; a clean starting file; exact tool versions; agreed success criteria; a recorded creation session; exported output; an independent reopening or use check. Choose a task such as turning a supplied game-stat table into a working filterable checklist.

**Hook variants:** “The assistant says it's finished. The real test is opening the file here.” Or: “One input, one usable output—every handoff included.”

**Timed narrative and shot map, 4–6 minutes:** 0:00–0:20 show the working destination artifact. 0:20–0:50 define the input and acceptance checks. 0:50–2:20 show meaningful creation steps, keeping corrections and interventions visible. 2:20–3:20 export, move or open the artifact through the real intended route. 3:20–4:20 test its functions and check source fidelity. 4:20 onward report total elapsed time, attempts and any remaining defect. Shorten waiting with labeled time jumps.

**Evidence:** save the original input, actual output and check results. A screenshot of success is insufficient when the promised result is an editable or interactive file. Never conceal manual repair behind an AI-only claim.

**Adaptations:** short videos focus on the decisive handoff and completed check; the full tutorial retains all prerequisites. Companion cards list the acceptance checks.

**CTA:** “What should every finished version of this include?” **Learning question:** does showing independent verification increase useful follow-up questions and saves?

### XF06 Audience challenge with recorded outcome

**Distinctive promise:** let an audience choice determine a bounded test, then deliver its result in the follow-up. The result episode must remain understandable without the invitation episode.

**Inputs:** a genuine viewer suggestion or clearly labeled pilot challenge; one achievable objective; written rules and budget; a recorded starting state; full attempts; outcome evidence. Choose the Gaming or AI lane by the main viewer benefit. Use one lead voice for an intentional crossover.

**Hook variants:** “You asked whether {challenge}. Here is the recorded attempt.” Or, before an audience exists: “Pilot challenge: can {bounded task} succeed under these rules?” Never invent a viewer request.

**Timed narrative and shot map, 90–120 seconds:** 0–8 show the decisive moment. 8–22 restate the challenge and rules. 22–48 show the first attempt and meaningful obstacle. 48–77 show the adjustment, including any intervention. 77–99 reveal the independently checked outcome, time and attempt count. 99–115 explain one lesson. A gaming challenge includes the featured game's spoken reception within this budget; extend the video when needed.

**Evidence:** preserve failed attempts and distinguish strategic choices from edited-out mistakes. A challenge inside a commercial game needs the normal game research. An original unreleased prototype has no invented external scores. The result may honestly be failure.

**Adaptations:** YouTube can carry the full attempt; short platforms get a self-contained result; Instagram's companion carousel can summarize rules and outcome. Ask a follow-up only after delivering this challenge's answer.

**CTA:** “Which single rule should change for the next attempt?” **Learning question:** do audience-chosen constraints produce better returning-viewer engagement than editor-chosen ones?
